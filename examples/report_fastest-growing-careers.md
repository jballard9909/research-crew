# Fastest Growing Career Paths: A Research Brief

The labor market over the next decade is being reshaped by three converging forces: the clean energy transition, the aging of the population, and the rapid diffusion of AI and digital technologies. Drawing on data from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), the World Economic Forum, and supporting research from institutions like McKinsey, Brookings, and the U.S. Department of Energy, this brief examines which occupations are growing fastest, why, what it takes to break into them, how they compare on compensation and stability, and where demand is concentrated geographically. Where source quality varies — for example, secondary summaries of primary data, or forward-looking modeled estimates — this is flagged accordingly.

## 1. Which Occupations Are Growing Fastest?

According to the BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook's 2024–34 projections (https://www.bls.gov/ooh/fastest-growing.htm and https://www.bls.gov/emp/tables/fastest-growing-occupations.htm), the occupations with the highest projected percentage growth are:

1. **Wind turbine service technicians** — ~49.9% growth, 2024 median pay $62,580, adding ~6,800 jobs
2. **Solar photovoltaic installers** — ~42.1% growth, 2024 median pay ~$48,800, adding ~12,000 jobs
3. **Nurse practitioners** — ~40.1% growth, 2024 median pay ~$126,260, adding ~128,400 jobs (the largest absolute job gain among the fastest-growers)
4. **Data scientists** — ~33.5% growth, 2024 median pay ~$108,020, adding ~82,500 jobs
5. **Information security analysts** — ~29% growth, 2024 median pay $124,910
6. **Medical and health services managers** — ~23% growth, 2024 median pay ~$117,960
7. **Physical therapist assistants** — ~22% growth
8. **Physician assistants, operations research analysts, and statisticians** round out the top ten

Exact figures shift slightly between BLS projection cycles (e.g., 2023–33 vs. 2024–34), so percentages should be treated as approximate to the cited cycle.

At the sector level, BLS's Monthly Labor Review (https://www.bls.gov/opub/mlr/2026/article/industry-and-occupational-employment-projections-overview.htm) identifies healthcare and social assistance as the fastest-growing major industry sector (8.4% growth, ~2.0 million new jobs) — the largest of any of the 20 major sectors tracked. CNBC's summary of BLS data (https://www.cnbc.com/2025/11/30/the-10-fastest-growing-jobs-of-the-next-decade-according-to-blsmany-can-pay-6-figures.html) corroborates that most top-10 fastest-growing roles for 2034 cluster in healthcare and information technology, though this is a secondary source and the BLS pages remain the authoritative reference.

Internationally, the World Economic Forum's *Future of Jobs Report 2025* (https://www.weforum.org/stories/2025/01/future-of-jobs-report-2025-the-fastest-growing-and-declining-jobs/; full report: https://reports.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Future_of_Jobs_Report_2025.pdf), based on a survey of 1,000+ global employers representing 14+ million workers, projects the fastest-growing global job categories through 2030 as: Big Data Specialists, FinTech Engineers, AI and Machine Learning Specialists, Software and Applications Developers, Security Management Specialists, Renewable Energy Engineers, Data Analysts and Scientists, Autonomous and Electric Vehicle Specialists, UI/UX Designers, and Internet of Things Specialists. Note that some secondary sources cite specific growth percentages (e.g., "Big Data Specialists +113%") attributed to this WEF report, but these figures could not be independently verified against the primary WEF document and should be treated cautiously.

## 2. What's Driving This Growth?

**Aging population and healthcare demand.** BLS analysis attributes healthcare occupation growth to demographic aging: "An aging population will fuel demand for workers in 3 of the 15 fastest growing industries; physical, occupational and speech therapists, and home healthcare" (https://www.bls.gov/opub/mlr/2026/article/industry-and-occupational-employment-projections-overview.htm). This pattern is consistent across BLS projection cycles going back years (e.g., https://www.bls.gov/opub/mlr/2015/article/overview-of-projections-to-2024.htm), which link rising chronic disease and an aging population to sustained demand for care-related occupations.

**Renewable energy transition.** The U.S. Department of Energy reports that clean energy jobs — including solar (+5.3%) and wind (+4.5%) — are growing more than twice as fast as overall U.S. employment (https://www.energy.gov/articles/doe-report-shows-clean-energy-jobs-grew-more-twice-rate-overall-us-employment). BLS's Beyond the Numbers series ties this directly to the physical expansion of renewable electricity generation capacity (https://www.bls.gov/opub/btn/volume-10/solar-and-wind-generation-occupations-a-look-at-the-next-decade.htm).

**AI adoption and digital transformation.** The WEF's Future of Jobs Report 2025 explicitly identifies AI, information-processing technologies, robotics, and autonomous systems as prominent drivers of both the fastest-growing and fastest-declining job categories (https://reports.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Future_of_Jobs_Report_2025.pdf). McKinsey Global Institute's research on generative AI (https://www.mckinsey.com/mgi/our-research/generative-ai-and-the-future-of-work-in-america) projects that generative AI will reshape labor demand, displacing some roles while creating new ones in tech and data fields. Brookings similarly finds that "more than 30% of all workers could see at least 50% of their occupation's tasks disrupted by generative AI" (https://www.brookings.edu/articles/generative-ai-the-american-worker-and-the-future-of-work/) — a relevant undercurrent behind rising demand for AI/ML specialists and the cybersecurity professionals who secure these systems. Note: these McKinsey and Brookings figures are modeled projections from respected research institutions, not administrative data, and should be read as informed estimates rather than certainties.

## 3. Education, Certification, and Accessibility for Career Changers

Entry requirements vary dramatically across these fast-growing fields, with important implications for accessibility to career changers:

**Nurse Practitioners — low-to-moderate accessibility.** Requires an RN license, an accredited graduate-level (master's or doctoral) nursing degree, a national certification exam, and state APRN licensure (https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/nurse-anesthetists-nurse-midwives-and-nurse-practitioners.htm; https://www.aanp.org/news-feed/explore-the-variety-of-career-paths-for-nurse-practitioners). The full pathway typically takes 6–8 years (https://www.nursingworld.org/content-hub/resources/becoming-a-nurse/what-is-nurse-practitioner/). Accelerated BSN and direct-entry MSN programs exist for career changers with unrelated bachelor's degrees, but the overall path remains long and clinically demanding.

**Wind Turbine Service Technicians — high accessibility.** The U.S. Department of Energy notes most technicians "learn their trade by attending a technical school. After completing a 2-year technical program, employers usually provide on-the-job training, typically lasting over 12 months" (https://www.energy.gov/cmei/systems/career-map-wind-technician). No license is required, though credentials like NCCER or OSHA 10 are recommended (https://skillpointe.com/news-and-advice/how-become-wind-turbine-technician-training-timing-and-more). This is one of the most accessible fast-growing fields, requiring only a high school diploma plus a short technical program.

**Solar Photovoltaic Installers — high accessibility.** BLS notes "on-the-job training usually lasts between 1 month and 1 year," and a four-year degree is not required (https://www.bls.gov/ooh/construction-and-extraction/solar-photovoltaic-installers.htm). Voluntary NABCEP certification can boost employability, requiring roughly 58 hours of advanced training plus field experience (https://www.nabcep.org/certifications/nabcep-board-certifications/).

**Data Scientists — moderate accessibility.** BLS states practitioners "typically need at least a bachelor's degree in mathematics, statistics, computer science, or a related field" (https://www.bls.gov/ooh/math/data-scientists.htm). Harvard Extension School notes immersive bootcamps teaching Python, SQL, statistics, and machine learning offer a viable alternative route for career changers (https://extension.harvard.edu/blog/data-science-artificial-intelligence-career-guide/), though some sources caution that bootcamp-only credentials face more employer resistance than traditional degrees.

**Information Security Analysts — moderate accessibility.** BLS indicates a bachelor's degree in computer science or a related field is generally required (https://www.bls.gov/ooh/computer-and-information-technology/information-security-analysts.htm), though industry certifications like CompTIA Security+ or CISSP can supplement or substitute for formal education, per industry sources such as a cybersecurity salary guide (https://www.kore1.com/cybersecurity-salary-guide/) — noted as a supplementary rather than authoritative source.

## 5. Geographic and Industry Demand, and the Remote Work Effect

**Renewable energy jobs are geographically concentrated and largely immune to remote work trends.** The World Resources Institute reports that "Texas is the top state for wind jobs, and California is the top state for solar and energy efficiency jobs," with Florida, Illinois, and Massachusetts also ranking highly (https://www.wri.org/insights/what-are-top-10-states-clean-energy-jobs). Yale Climate Connections notes that "just two states hold one-third of the jobs in clean electricity generation: California and Texas" (https://yaleclimateconnections.org/2024/10/jobs-in-wind-solar-and-energy-storage-are-booming-is-your-state-keeping-up/). BLS confirms Texas, Iowa, and California lead in wind generating capacity, though wind jobs exist in nearly every state (https://www.bls.gov/green/wind_energy/). Because these are hands-on, infrastructure-based roles, remote work trends have essentially no effect on their geographic distribution.

**Healthcare demand tracks population, with limited remote flexibility.** BLS confirms healthcare/social assistance is the fastest-growing sector overall (https://www.bls.gov/opub/mlr/2026/article/industry-and-occupational-employment-projections-overview.htm), but clinical roles like nurse practitioners require in-person patient care, which limits how much remote work can redistribute this demand geographically. (Telehealth's role in enabling hybrid NP practice is a plausible factor but was not independently confirmed with a strong source in this research.)

**Tech and data roles are the most remote-flexible, decoupling talent from geography.** Industry data cited by Gable.to indicates "technology leads at 47% fully remote, followed by finance and insurance at 40%" (https://www.gable.to/blog/post/work-from-home-statistics) — though this is an industry blog aggregating survey data, not a government or peer-reviewed source. Robert Half notes cybersecurity and data & analytics roles are frequently among the most remote-friendly categories, but also flags that the remote landscape is currently in flux, citing a shift from 65% to 87% fully in-office roles in one proprietary dataset between Q4 2025 and Q2 2026 (https://www.roberthalf.com/us/en/insights/research/remote-work-statistics-and-trends). Forbes Advisor, citing Upwork data, estimated 32.6 million Americans (~22% of the workforce) would work remotely by 2025, with technology and data roles disproportionately represented (https://www.forbes.com/advisor/business/remote-work-statistics/).

**Overall implication:** Remote work trends are increasingly decoupling tech-driven fast-growing roles (data scientists, information security analysts, AI/ML specialists) from specific metro hubs, enabling employers to draw from a distributed talent pool. By contrast, renewable energy trade jobs and direct-care healthcare roles remain tied to the physical locations of infrastructure and patient populations, and are largely unaffected by remote work trends.
