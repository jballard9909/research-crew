# The Economic Impact of Remote Work: A Research Brief

The shift to remote and hybrid work since 2020 has reshaped major dimensions of the U.S. economy — from how productively companies operate, to the fate of downtown office towers, to where people choose to live, how they spend their money, and what they earn. Drawing on government data (BLS, Federal Reserve, Congressional Research Service), peer-reviewed and NBER academic research, and established research institutions, this brief synthesizes the evidence across five key questions. Where only industry surveys or lower-confidence sources are available, this is noted explicitly.

---

## 1. How has the shift to remote work affected productivity and output across different industries and job functions?

The evidence is mixed and depends heavily on methodology, industry, and whether work is fully remote or hybrid.

The **U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics** analyzed 61 detailed private-sector industries and found a **positive correlation between the rise in remote work and total factor productivity (TFP) growth** between 2019 and 2022. BLS noted that remote work "substantially contributed to productivity growth during the pandemic," partly by reducing job turnover as job satisfaction rose, lowering firms' hiring and training costs ([bls.gov](https://www.bls.gov/opub/btn/volume-13/remote-work-productivity.htm); [related data](https://www.bls.gov/productivity/articles-and-research/remote-work/productivity-and-remote-work.htm)).

By contrast, **Stanford SIEPR** research (Bloom, Barrero, Davis), published in the *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, found that **fully remote work is associated with about 10% lower productivity than fully in-person work**, driven by communication barriers, mentoring difficulties, and reduced innovation. However, **hybrid work (2–3 days remote) showed neutral-to-positive productivity effects** and significantly improved employee retention without harming performance ([siepr.stanford.edu](https://siepr.stanford.edu/publications/working-paper/evolution-working-home); [full paper](https://wfhresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/SIEPR1.pdf); [related Nature paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07500-2)).

A firm-level study in the **University of Chicago Journals** found that when a company shifted to full remote work, **hours worked increased but output declined slightly, with total factor productivity falling 8%–19%**, largely attributed to reduced informal mentoring and coordination ([journals.uchicago.edu](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/721803)).

The **Congressional Research Service** synthesizes these findings, noting that productivity effects vary by occupation — highly collaborative or supervision-intensive functions see declines under full remote arrangements, while independent, computer-based tasks see gains or neutral effects ([congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R48528)).

A lower-confidence, survey-based study by **Great Place to Work** (800,000+ employees) found "stable or improved productivity" after remote transitions — useful corroborating evidence, but weighted below the government and academic sources above ([greatplacetowork.com](https://www.greatplacetowork.com/resources/blog/remote-work-productivity-study-finds-surprising-reality-2-year-study)).

**Bottom line:** Aggregate government and macro-level academic data point to productivity gains associated with remote/hybrid work (via retention and lower overhead), while firm-level studies show fully remote arrangements can reduce output by roughly 8–20% in collaboration-heavy contexts. Hybrid arrangements are the most consistently positive across sources.

## 2. What impact has remote work had on commercial real estate demand, urban commercial districts, and property values in major cities?

**Moody's Analytics** data shows the U.S. office vacancy rate hit a **record high of 20.1% in Q2 2024**, breaking 20% for the first time ever, and climbed further to **20.4% by the end of 2024**. Moody's also documents a divergence between downtown and suburban markets, with some downtown vacancy rates (e.g., Austin, Denver, San Francisco) exceeding 25–30% ([moodys.com](https://www.moodys.com/web/en/us/insights/data-stories/us-commercial-real-estate-vacancies-downtown-vs-suburbs.html); [Q4 2024 update](https://www.moodyscre.com/insights/cre-trends/q4-2024-preliminary-trend-announcement/)).

**NBER research** (Ramani & Bloom, "The Donut Effect of Covid-19 on Cities") found strong evidence of a **"donut effect"** — economic activity, population, and real estate demand shifting from dense city centers to suburban rings, with the effect strongest in the largest, highest-density cities and closely tied to the geography of remote-work potential ([nber.org](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28876); [full PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28876/w28876.pdf)). A companion global study published in **PNAS** confirms this pattern internationally ([pnas.org](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2408930121)).

Another **NBER working paper** (Van Nieuwerburgh et al., "Work from Home and the Office Real Estate Apocalypse") estimates significant destruction of office property value tied to remote work, with implications for municipal tax revenue since office/retail real estate taxes constitute a large share of city budgets — e.g., roughly 35% of NYC's total tax revenue in the study period ([nber.org](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w30662/w30662.pdf)).

The **NYC Office of the Comptroller** reports that office vacancy and availability rose substantially post-pandemic, with associated declines in office property valuations and tax revenue — though more recent 2024–2025 reports show a partial "boom loop" recovery in select high-quality ("trophy") office assets, complicating the simple "doom loop" narrative ([comptroller.nyc.gov](https://comptroller.nyc.gov/reports/nycs-office-market-doom-loop-or-boom-loop/)).

**Pew Charitable Trusts**, in a case study of Atlanta, Boston, Dallas, Denver, and Milwaukee, found that office vacancies are straining municipal budgets reliant on commercial property tax revenue, though effects vary significantly by city depending on tax structure diversity — cities more reliant on residential property tax revenue are less exposed ([pew.org](https://www.pew.org/en/research-and-analysis/reports/2026/05/the-remote-work-challenge-lessons-from-5-cities); [full PDF](https://www.pew.org/-/media/assets/2026/05/sfp/the-remote-work-challenge-lessons-from-5-cities.pdf)).

**Bottom line:** Multiple authoritative sources converge on record-high office vacancy rates (~20% nationally, higher in tech-heavy metros), a "donut effect" of activity shifting from downtowns to suburbs, and material declines in commercial property values with knock-on effects for municipal tax revenue — though some markets show signs of partial stabilization in top-tier office assets.

## 3. How has remote work influenced regional labor markets, including migration patterns from high-cost urban centers to smaller cities and rural areas, and what effects has this had on local economies?

An **NBER working paper** ("The Geography of Remote Work," Althoff, Eckert, Ganapati, Walsh) found that big, dense cities specialize in high-skill service jobs that are more remote-work-compatible, and that these cities saw significantly larger outflows of business-service workers than smaller cities. Critically, the study found this shift **hurt local service economies**: "low-skill consumer-service workers in big cities lost more hours" as high earners left or reduced their in-person presence, taking their spending with them ([nber.org PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29181/w29181.pdf); [NBER Digest summary](https://www.nber.org/digest/202112/remote-work-high-skill-workers-hurt-local-service-economies)).

The **NBER "Donut Effect"** paper documents migration out of large, expensive urban cores toward suburbs and smaller metro areas, strongest among higher-income, remote-capable workers, citing moving-company data showing "a movement of mostly high-income people to smaller, less costly cities" ([nber.org](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28876/revisions/w28876.rev0.pdf)).

The **Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia** found that **remote households spend over 7% more on housing than comparable non-remote households** within the same commuting zone, and that remote work retains a positive association with local household income effects years later, with some positive income spillovers to neighboring areas ([philadelphiafed.org](https://www.philadelphiafed.org/the-economy/regional-economics/the-geographic-and-economic-implications-of-working-from-home)).

The **Congressional Research Service** notes that remote work has enabled geographic redistribution of jobs and income toward smaller cities and rural areas, but cautions that benefits are unevenly distributed and concentrated among higher-education, higher-income remote-capable workers ([congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R48528)).

The **Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City** cautions that rural economic developers' strategies to attract remote workers may have mixed effects — while inflows of remote workers can boost local spending, they may also **exacerbate existing labor shortages** for local employers by increasing housing costs and drawing workers away from local jobs ([kansascityfed.org](https://www.kansascityfed.org/community/community-connections/does-a-remote-work-strategy-make-sense-for-rural-economic-developers/)).

A lower-confidence, non-peer-reviewed report from the **Center on Rural Innovation** documents "Zoom Town" phenomena and state-level incentive programs (e.g., Arkansas's "Life Works Here") attracting remote workers to small towns — useful descriptive/anecdotal evidence, weighted below the NBER/Fed sources ([ruralinnovation.us](https://ruralinnovation.us/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Remote-Work_122721.pdf)).

**Bottom line:** Rigorous NBER and Federal Reserve research confirms a clear migration pattern of higher-income, remote-capable workers from large, expensive cities to smaller metros, suburbs, and rural areas. This benefits receiving communities via increased household income and spending, but hurts consumer-service workers left behind in large cities who lose hours/income as high earners' in-person spending declines, and can create new housing-cost pressures and labor shortages in receiving rural/small-city labor markets.

## 4. What are the effects of remote work on household spending patterns, including reduced commuting costs, changes in consumer spending on business attire, dining, and other commute-related expenses?

An **NBER working paper** (Stanton & Tiwari, "Housing Consumption and the Cost of Remote Work") found that remote workers have shifted spending toward housing — the **expenditure share on housing is about seven percent higher for remote households** compared to non-remote households, as workers substitute commuting savings and flexibility into larger or better homes, often farther from urban cores ([nber.org](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28483); [full PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28483/w28483.pdf)).

This is corroborated by the **Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia**, which similarly found remote households spend over 7% more on housing within the same commuting zone than comparable non-remote households ([philadelphiafed.org](https://www.philadelphiafed.org/the-economy/regional-economics/the-geographic-and-economic-implications-of-working-from-home)).

The widely-cited **NBER/WFH Research paper** by Barrero, Bloom, and Davis ("Why Working from Home Will Stick") documents that the shift to WFH **alters the geography of consumer spending**, redirecting spending away from central business districts (restaurants, retail, services near offices) toward residential neighborhoods and suburbs ([ideas.repec.org](https://ideas.repec.org/p/nbr/nberwo/28731.html); [full text](https://stevenjdavis.com/s/Why-Working-From-Home-Will-Stick-5-February-2026.pdf)).

A peer-reviewed literature review in **ScienceDirect**, citing Barrero et al. (2021), notes projections that WFH would reduce consumer spending by an estimated **13% in Manhattan and 4.6% in San Francisco**, reflecting the loss of office-worker foot traffic in central business districts. This is flagged as a projection from a single study line rather than a directly re-verified government statistic, though it is sourced through a peer-reviewed review ([sciencedirect.com](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0927537123001379)).

A peer-reviewed article on **PMC/NIH** cites Citrix survey-based estimates that working remotely an average of 2.4 days/week could generate **cost savings of $44.4 billion on commuting** (tickets/fuel) — based on a third-party industry survey embedded in a peer-reviewed article, so moderate confidence ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8138437/)).

A lower-confidence industry survey (Owl Labs, via Harvard FCU) estimates in-office workers spend on average ~$15/day on commuting and ~$14.25/day on lunch — illustrative figures from a private survey, not an academic or government dataset ([harvardfcu.org](https://harvardfcu.org/blog/the-costs-of-in-office-work-vs-remote-work/)).

**Bottom line:** The strongest evidence (NBER, Federal Reserve) shows remote work has shifted household spending decisively toward housing (~7% more of income), and away from central-business-district-adjacent spending (dining, retail, apparel near offices), with estimated consumer spending declines in specific downtown areas (e.g., Manhattan, SF) in the high single-to-low double digits. Commuting-cost savings are well-documented directionally, though specific dollar figures often originate from industry surveys rather than government statistics.

## 5. How does remote work affect wage structures and labor market competition, including geographic pay disparities, access to broader talent pools for employers, and income effects for workers in different regions?

A **Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco** working paper ("The Work-from-Home Wage Premium") finds that **workers who work from home earn on average 12% higher hourly wages than fully on-site workers**. Importantly, the study notes nearly half of this premium is explained by demographic/human-capital factors (age, gender, tenure) rather than remote work itself causally raising pay ([frbsf.org](https://www.frbsf.org/research-and-insights/publications/working-papers/2026/02/the-work-from-home-wage-premium/); [Fortune coverage](https://fortune.com/2026/02/26/remote-hybrid-employees-earning-more-in-person-colleagues-france-san-francisco-fed-study/)).

The **Congressional Research Service** cites data showing the wage premium for all remote workers rose in 2020 and 2021, and discusses how remote work is associated with increased wages in some sectors (management, business operations) but potentially lower relative wages in others (e.g., health care support), reflecting occupational sorting rather than a uniform effect ([congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R48528)).

The **ADP Research Institute**, using large administrative payroll datasets, finds that employees newly hired into "long-distance" (remote, geographically distant from employer) positions received **larger average pay gains than similar job-switchers who took local positions** — evidence that remote work has widened employers' access to talent pools and increased wage competition for in-demand remote-capable workers ([adpresearch.com](https://www.adpresearch.com/research/long-distance-work-and-compensation)).

A **CEPR/Sheffield discussion paper** (De Fraja, Matheson, et al., "Remote Work and Compensation Inequality"), using original survey data, finds that the option to work remotely itself carries compensating-differential effects on pay, and that remote work is reshaping compensation inequality patterns — workers who value remote work options sometimes accept a "pay to work from home" tradeoff, which can offset headline wage premiums ([eprints.whiterose.ac.uk](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/218871/1/Binder2024008.pdf)).

Industry/HR practice data from **SHRM** (moderate confidence, not academic) indicate over half (55%) of organizations use city/metro-area cost-of-labor indices to set differentiated pay for remote workers, showing employers are actively implementing location-based pay policies rather than fully "leveling" wages nationally ([shrm.org](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/news/benefits-compensation/remote-workers-expect-pay-to-reflect-locations)).

**Bottom line:** Central bank research shows a real but partly-compositional wage premium (~12%) for remote workers, roughly half attributable to worker characteristics rather than remote status itself. Administrative payroll data show remote work has expanded employers' geographic talent pools and increased pay for workers taking geographically distant positions. At the same time, many employers continue to apply geographic/cost-of-labor pay differentials, meaning full wage convergence across regions has not occurred — remote work has increased competition and pay opportunities for remote-capable workers without fully eliminating regional pay disparities.
