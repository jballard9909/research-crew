# AI's Impact on Primary and Secondary Schools: A Research Brief

Artificial intelligence is moving into K-12 classrooms faster than research can rigorously evaluate it. Teacher adoption of AI tools has surged in just two years, districts are scrambling to write policy, and federal, state, and international bodies are issuing guidance to manage the risks. Yet the evidence base on whether AI actually improves learning outcomes remains thin, and significant concerns persist around data privacy, bias, equity of access, and the changing nature of teachers' work. This brief synthesizes the sourced research findings across five key questions: current classroom use, measurable effects on learning, risks and challenges, the evolving teacher role, and emerging policy frameworks.

---

## 1. How is AI currently being used in primary and secondary classrooms to support teaching and personalize student learning?

Adoption is rising quickly but remains uneven across grade levels and time. RAND Corporation's nationally representative survey of K–12 teachers found that as of fall 2023, only 18% of teachers reported using AI tools for teaching, with another 15% having tried it at least once; middle and high school teachers were more likely to use it than elementary teachers ([RAND Corporation](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA956-21.html)).

Usage then grew dramatically: a follow-up RAND report found that by the 2024–25 school year, roughly 60% of teachers said they used AI, saving an estimated up to six hours of work per week, and 48% of districts reported having trained teachers on AI use, up from 23% the prior year ([RAND](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA956-31.html); also reported by [The 74](https://www.the74million.org/article/survey-60-of-teachers-used-ai-this-year-and-saved-up-to-6-hours-of-work-a-week/) and [Education Week](https://www.edweek.org/technology/more-teachers-than-ever-before-are-trained-on-ai-are-they-ready-to-use-it/2025/04)).

Common current classroom uses identified in these surveys and in a U.S. Department of Education report include: adaptive/personalized practice software that adjusts difficulty to student performance, AI tutoring assistants (such as Khan Academy's "Khanmigo," piloted in districts like Newark, NJ and Hobart, IN), automated grading and feedback tools, lesson- and resource-planning assistance for teachers, and differentiated content generation for students at different ability levels ([U.S. Department of Education](https://www.ed.gov/sites/ed/files/documents/ai-report/ai-report.pdf)).

Real-world uptake of even well-resourced tools can be inconsistent. Khan Academy's own blog notes that Sal Khan has publicly acknowledged AI tutoring was "a non-event" for many students because usage was inconsistent even where the tool was available ([Khan Academy Blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/learning-in-the-open-what-ai-is-and-isnt-changing/)).

## 2. What measurable effects does AI integration have on student academic performance, engagement, and skill development across different age groups and subjects?

The rigorous evidence base is still thin despite a large volume of published research. A Stanford SCALE research brief reviewed the K-12 AI research literature and found that of over 800 academic papers in their repository on AI in K-12 education, only about 20 met a rigorous, causal-evidence bar such as randomized controlled trials or strong quasi-experimental designs. The brief cautions that AI is arriving in classrooms faster than research can rigorously evaluate it ([Stanford SCALE](https://scale.stanford.edu/research-in-action/understanding-evidence-base-ai-k12-education)).

Within that rigorous subset, students often perform better on narrow, immediate tasks — such as math practice problems, programming assignments, or writing exercises — when using AI tools, but evidence on retention, deeper skill transfer, and long-term academic performance is much weaker and mixed (same Stanford SCALE source).

A related synthesis from KnowledgeWorks describes this as an "expertise paradox": AI tools can improve immediate task performance, but there is emerging concern that heavy reliance may reduce development of foundational skills and independent problem-solving over time ([KnowledgeWorks](https://knowledgeworks.org/resources/expertise-paradox-work-k-12-education/)).

Peer-reviewed literature reviews report broadly positive findings on personalized learning, academic outcomes, and engagement, but these are largely narrative/systematic reviews of self-reported and correlational studies rather than causal evidence, and many focus on higher education rather than K-12 specifically ([MDPI, Education Sciences](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/15/3/343); [PMC/NCBI](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11830699/)).

A caution flag: some widely-circulated statistics — such as claims of "54% higher test scores" or "10x more engagement" from AI-powered learning environments — originate from vendor/marketing content rather than primary research and should be treated with low confidence ([engageli.com](https://www.engageli.com/blog/ai-in-education-statistics)).

## 3. What challenges and risks—such as data privacy, algorithmic bias, over-reliance on technology, and equitable access—arise from implementing AI tools in K-12 schools?

A peer-reviewed article on ethical challenges in K-12 AI identifies the biggest risks as: AI perpetuating existing systemic bias and discrimination through algorithms trained on historical/biased data; data privacy and surveillance concerns from continuous student data collection; and unclear accountability when AI systems make consequential decisions about students ([PMC/NCBI](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8455229/)).

Stanford Law School notes that AI algorithms trained on historical educational data risk exacerbating racial disparities, since past patterns of discrimination and unequal outcomes can be embedded and reproduced in AI-driven decisions such as placement, discipline predictions, or resource allocation ([Stanford Law School](https://law.stanford.edu/2024/06/29/how-will-ai-impact-racial-disparities-in-education/)).

The Center for Democracy & Technology warns that without safeguards, algorithmic systems in education can pose risks to student privacy, free expression, and civil rights, and calls for equity- and fairness-focused design when student data is used ([CDT](https://cdt.org/insights/algorithmic-systems-in-education-incorporating-equity-and-fairness-when-using-student-data/)).

On equitable access, a persistent "digital divide" risks being widened by AI adoption. GovTech reporting cites that roughly 40% of children in low-income households lack internet access outside of school, meaning AI-based personalized/adaptive tools that require home connectivity may disproportionately benefit already-advantaged students ([GovTech](https://www.govtech.com/education/will-ai-in-schools-widen-the-digital-divide)). Relatedly, IDRA reports that only 50% of high-poverty schools have dedicated high-speed internet lines compared to 72% of low-poverty schools — an infrastructure gap directly relevant to equitable AI implementation ([IDRA](https://www.idra.org/resource-center/bridging-the-digital-divide-in-our-schools/)).

The U.S. Department of Education's own report explicitly flags data privacy and data security risks alongside newer risks such as "scaling pattern detectors and automation" that could embed bias at scale ([U.S. Dept of Education](https://www.ed.gov/sites/ed/files/documents/ai-report/ai-report.pdf)).

## 4. How is AI reshaping the role of teachers, including their workload, instructional practices, and required training or professional development?

A systematic review published in *Computers and Education: Artificial Intelligence* examined research from 2015–2024 on teachers' use of AI in both instruction and their own professional development, finding growing but uneven integration and a need for structured PD models ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666920X24001589)).

Formal training is expanding rapidly: RAND's district-level survey found that 48% of districts had trained teachers on AI use by fall 2024, up from 23% in fall 2023 — a 25-percentage-point jump in one year — though this still leaves roughly half of districts without formal training programs ([RAND](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA956-31.html)).

On workload, an England-based study across six secondary schools found that with clear guidance, teachers using AI reduced lesson- and resource-planning time by approximately 31% (reported via [Dr. Rod Lane's analysis on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/does-ai-actually-reduce-teacher-workload-what-evidence-dr-rod-lane-axmde); note this is secondary commentary on a study, so the specific figure should be treated with some caution). Separately, RAND survey data reported by The 74 found that during 2024–25, 68% of teachers reported using AI, and among teachers at schools with a formal AI policy, up to six hours per week were reportedly saved on tasks like grading and planning ([The 74](https://www.the74million.org/article/survey-60-of-teachers-used-ai-this-year-and-saved-up-to-6-hours-of-work-a-week/)).

The National Education Association's Task Force on Artificial Intelligence in Education produced a formal report addressing how AI is changing instructional practice and teacher roles, emphasizing that AI should support rather than replace teacher judgment, and calling for substantial investment in teacher training ([NEA](https://www.nea.org/sites/default/files/2024-10/nea-ai-task-force-report-2024.pdf)).

## 5. What policies, guidelines, or ethical frameworks are being developed by schools, districts, and governments to regulate AI use in primary and secondary education?

**Federal level (U.S.):** The U.S. Department of Education released formal guidance, "Artificial Intelligence and the Future of Teaching and Learning: Insights and Recommendations," organized around principles including "Center People, Not Technology," "Advance Equity," and "Ensure Safety, Ethics, and Transparency." The Department later also issued a 74-page AI toolkit for K-12 leaders and additional nondiscrimination/civil rights resources ([U.S. Dept of Education](https://www.ed.gov/sites/ed/files/documents/ai-report/ai-report.pdf); [press release](https://www.ed.gov/about/news/press-release/us-department-of-education-issues-guidance-artificial-intelligence-use-schools-proposes-additional-supplemental-priority); [K-12 Dive](https://www.k12dive.com/news/education-department-ai-guidance-school-leaders/731038/)).

**State level:** As of recent tracking, 33–35 U.S. states have issued official state-level AI guidance or policy for K-12 schools, compiled by AI for Education ([aiforeducation.io](https://www.aiforeducation.io/ai-resources/state-ai-guidance)) and tracked by [Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/AI_guidance_issued_by_state_departments_of_education). Ohio became the first U.S. state to require every K-12 district to adopt a formal AI use policy, either a state model policy or a locally developed one, according to the National Association of State Boards of Education ([NASBE](https://www.nasbe.org/states-take-next-steps-on-governing-ai-use-in-schools/)). Tennessee enacted Public Chapter 550 (2024), requiring every K-12 district, charter school, and public university in the state to adopt its own AI policy ([Playlab Learning Hub](https://learn.playlab.ai/resources/aistatepolicies)).

Legislative momentum is accelerating: the Center for Democracy & Technology found that 53 AI-in-education bills were proposed across 21 U.S. states during the 2025 legislative session, with 51 specifically targeting K-12 use ([CDT](https://cdt.org/insights/states-focused-on-responsible-use-of-ai-in-education-during-the-2025-legislative-session/)).

**International/global framework:** UNESCO published "Guidance for Generative AI in Education and Research," the first global guidance of its kind, offering concrete recommendations to policymakers and institutions on regulating GenAI tools to protect human agency, promote equity, and support long-term policy planning. This built on UNESCO's earlier 2021 publication, "AI and Education: Guidance for Policy-Makers" ([UNESCO](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research); [UNESCO 2021](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/ai-and-education-guidance-policy-makers)).

**District level example:** New York City Public Schools published explicit guidance stating that AI tools must be used with human oversight, that "AI supports—it never replaces—educator decision-making," and restricting entry of personal student information into unapproved AI tools ([NYC Public Schools](https://www.schools.nyc.gov/about-us/vision-and-mission/artificial-intelligence/guidance-on-artificial-intelligence)).

Nonprofit and practitioner frameworks such as CoSN (Consortium for School Networking) and TeachAI also provide widely used toolkits and ethical frameworks that many districts reference when building local policy ([CoSN](https://www.cosn.org/ai/); [TeachAI](https://www.teachai.org/toolkit)).
