# Middle School Student Engagement in General Music Class: A Research Brief

Middle school general music classrooms face a well-documented engagement challenge: participation and enthusiasm often decline as students move through grades 6–8, even as students bring diverse preferences shaped by gender, prior training, and cultural background. This brief synthesizes peer-reviewed research (supplemented cautiously by a small number of practitioner sources) to answer five questions about what activities, instructional strategies, and curricular choices most engage middle schoolers in general music class. Where the underlying research draws on elementary, high school, or international samples rather than U.S. middle schoolers specifically, this is noted, as such findings are extrapolated with appropriate caution.

## 1. Which musical activities do middle school students find most enjoyable, and how does engagement vary across activity types?

The clearest and most consistent finding across decades of research is that **hands-on, active activities — especially playing instruments — are rated far above passive or abstract activities like composing or talking about music.**

- The foundational study by Bowles (1998) asked students to choose a favorite among six activity types (singing, dancing/movement, listening, composing, playing instruments, talking about music). **Playing instruments was the top choice for 50% of students**, far ahead of dancing/movement (15%) and singing (14%). Listening was the favorite for 11% of students, while only **6% chose composing** ([JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/3345623); [University of Maryland summary](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/bitstreams/f6c720e5-2b38-47af-9b67-87ad690e93c5/download)).
- A more recent replication and extension of this work, Culp & Davis (2023), found that **playing instruments remained highly popular**, with free creative movement/dance and playing drums also ranking among the top-rated activities for upper-elementary students ([Journal of General Music Education](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/27527646231162324)).
- A follow-up synthesis by Davis & Culp (2024) confirms that playing instruments has remained a top preference across multiple decades of research (citing Bowles 1998, Culp & Davis 2023, and Nolin 1973), while also noting that general interest in music class **tends to decline as students approach adolescence**, particularly when activities are not personally meaningful to students ([Journal of General Music Education](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/27527646231188089)).
- Practitioner sources reinforce similar practical takeaways — ensemble playing, rhythm games, and call-and-response as engaging activities — though these should be read as professional opinion rather than research evidence ([MakeMusic blog](https://www.makemusic.com/blog/student-engagement-in-music-class-best-strategies-and-benefits/)).

**Caveat:** Much of the most rigorous evidence on activity preference comes from elementary or upper-elementary samples rather than middle school specifically, so findings should be extrapolated to grades 6–8 with some caution.

## 2. How do gender, age/grade level, and prior musical training influence music activity preferences?

**Gender** shapes instrument and activity preference in patterned, stereotype-consistent ways. A 2025 study of Italian primary and middle school students found that **girls gravitate toward clarinet, flute, and violin, while boys gravitate toward drums and other "masculine-coded" instruments**, reflecting persistent gender stereotyping in instrument choice ([Concina et al., 2025, MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/15/4/474)). This corroborates earlier research finding that flute, clarinet, and oboe are femininely coded while brass and percussion are masculinely coded among fifth graders — a pattern the Concina study shows persists into middle school ([Wiedenfeld, 2012, ERIC](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1102257.pdf)). A professional-association summary notes that such gender perceptions extend beyond instrument choice to affect "beliefs about creativity, ensemble leadership, and rates of participation" ([NAfME blog](https://nafme.org/blog/embracing-human-difference-in-music-education/)).

**Age/grade level** is strongly associated with declining engagement. Research tracking musical activity dropout found that **children aged 11–15 — squarely the middle-school age range — leave music-making activities at markedly higher rates than other age groups**, marking a critical window of declining engagement ([Ruth, 2021, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8612519/)). A National Endowment for the Arts–affiliated research brief examining over 31,000 students found that **only 22% ever enrolled in a middle-school music class, with participation lowest in 8th grade** ([NEA/George Mason research brief](https://www.arts.gov/sites/default/files/Research-Art-Works-GeorgeMason4.pdf)). Relatedly, research on general music as a required course found that the percentage of middle schools offering a band elective rises from 68% by 5th grade to 99% by 8th grade — meaning students increasingly self-select out of general music into ensemble electives as they age, reshaping who remains in general music and their engagement profile ([Cronenberg, 2020, RMLE Online](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19404476.2020.1818020)). Davis & Culp's synthesis likewise notes that "many students become less interested in school music as they mature" ([Journal of General Music Education](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/27527646231188089)).

**Prior musical training:** Direct middle-school-specific research isolating prior training as a moderator of general-music engagement was sparse in this search. A systematic literature review of classroom music motivation organizes findings around expectancy-value theory, self-determination theory, mastery motivation, and achievement goal theory; expectancy-value theory implies that students with prior musical competence report higher perceived competence, which theoretically predicts higher engagement — though this is an inference from theoretical framework rather than a direct empirical finding specific to middle schoolers, and should be treated as weaker evidence ([Kiss, Oo, Biró & Józsa, 2025, MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/15/7/862)).

## 3. What role does technology play in increasing engagement compared to traditional instructional methods?

Available evidence suggests technology can boost engagement and ownership, though direct middle-school-specific experimental studies are limited.

- A dissertation study of high school students using GarageBand for iPad found that mobile music technology **increased student engagement and ownership in composition tasks** compared to traditional notation-based composition ([Sabet, 2019, Rutgers](https://rucore.libraries.rutgers.edu/rutgers-lib/61575/)) — though this sample is high school rather than middle school.
- A 2025 review of digital tools in elementary music education found that "most studies reported positive effects of using digital tools on musical skills," and that in several studies these interventions **"not only improved measured musical skills but also had significant impacts on motivation and engagement"** ([Journal, Sage 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/20592043251363338)) — again, an elementary-focused sample requiring cautious extrapolation to middle school.
- A bibliometric analysis published in *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* documents the growing volume of research on technology in music education, but is a citation-analysis rather than a direct engagement study, so it is useful mainly for showing the field's expanding interest rather than for effect-size claims ([Nature, 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-04616-2)).
- Relatedly, a controlled study comparing computer-mediated composition in popular versus classical music conditions found generally higher engagement in the technology-supported popular-music condition (see Question 4 for detail) ([Chen & O'Neill, 2020](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14613808.2020.1737924)).

**Caveat:** Direct peer-reviewed experimental studies isolating technology's effect specifically within middle school general music classrooms were limited in this search; the findings above are drawn substantially from adjacent age groups (elementary, high school) and should be extrapolated to middle school with caution.

## 4. How do preferences for popular/self-selected music compare with engagement in traditional/classical curricula?

The available evidence converges on the finding that **popular, self-selected, or culturally relevant music is associated with higher engagement than traditional/classical curricula**, though much of this evidence relies on teacher perception rather than experimentally controlled student measures.

- The strongest controlled comparison found in this research directly examined 44 students composing in either a popular or classical music condition using computer-mediated tools. The study found that **"the popular music group had a higher level of engagement than the classical music group, and they thought about learning to compose music even [more outside class]."** Popular music students also reported greater perceived improvement in their compositional competence ([Chen & O'Neill, 2020, Music Education Research](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14613808.2020.1737924); [ERIC record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1251292)).
- A national study of "Modern Band" (popular-music-based curriculum) programs found that teachers **perceived modern band as expanding access to music education by engaging more students**, and it increased teachers' own commitment to teaching ([Knapp & Davis, 2022, Journal of Research in Music Education](https://www.jstor.org/stable/48678460)).
- A related descriptive study found that **76% of teachers reported students were more engaged in music class since incorporating modern band/popular music curricula** — though this is teacher-perception data rather than direct student self-report, a notable methodological limitation ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/329720582_Modern_band_A_descriptive_study_of_teacher_perceptions)).
- Work on student voice and choice in modern band curriculum development emphasizes that centering student voice/choice and popular/self-selected music **improves buy-in, particularly for students from backgrounds underrepresented in traditional band, orchestra, and choir** ([Clauhs, Cremata, et al., Journal of Popular Music Education](https://www.ithaca.edu/sites/default/files/2021-03/2020-jpme-student-voice-and-choice.pdf)).

## 5. What instructional strategies or classroom structures are most strongly associated with sustained student interest and participation?

The most consistent peer-reviewed evidence points to **autonomy-supportive teaching, student voice/choice, and active/hands-on participatory structures** as the strategies most robustly linked to sustained engagement.

- A 2025 study of music students found that **autonomy-supportive teaching significantly predicts flow experience (deep engagement/immersion) and collaborative learning, which in turn mediate improvements in student well-being** ([Xu & Li, 2025, Acta Psychologica](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0001691825001404); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40023919/)). This sample was Chinese music students, not U.S. middle schoolers specifically, so generalizability should be qualified.
- Earlier, highly-cited research similarly found that **autonomy-supportive teaching practices and teachers' passion transmission were strongly linked to greater student engagement and motivation** ([Bonneville-Roussy et al., 2020, ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id)).
- Davis & Culp's practice-focused synthesis explicitly recommends **using student voice and choice to reshape curriculum** as a strategy for sustaining engagement in general music, based on their own empirical findings on activity preferences ([Journal of General Music Education](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/27527646231188089)).
- Qualitative research on modern band curriculum development found that centering **student voice/choice alongside hands-on, small-ensemble popular music performance** correlates with sustained participation, especially among students historically underrepresented in traditional ensembles ([Clauhs et al., Ithaca College](https://www.ithaca.edu/sites/default/files/2021-03/2020-jpme-student-voice-and-choice.pdf)).
- A study of 120 middle school students found that **active musical participation (as opposed to passive listening) is linked to greater school connectedness and hopeful future expectations**, supporting participatory over passive instructional structures ([Ilari & Cho, 2023, Frontiers in Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9889371/); [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.1056542/full)).
- The systematic review of classroom music motivation identifies self-determination theory (autonomy, competence, relatedness) as one of four core frameworks explaining music motivation, theoretically supporting choice-based and collaborative structures as engagement drivers ([Kiss et al., 2025, MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/15/7/862)).
- Practitioner sources (NAfME, MakeMusic) similarly recommend small-group collaboration, hands-on performance, and project-based learning, consistent with the peer-reviewed findings above, though these are professional-association and blog commentary rather than original research ([MakeMusic blog](https://www.makemusic.com/blog/student-engagement-in-music-class-best-strategies-and-benefits/); [NAfME blog](https://nafme.org/blog/creating-middle-school-general-music-curriculum-scratch/)).

**Caveat:** Much of the strongest causal evidence (e.g., the autonomy-support/flow research) comes from non-U.S. or non-middle-school-specific samples, so these findings should be extrapolated to the middle school general music context with some caution.
